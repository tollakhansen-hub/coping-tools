# My body won't settle — focused evidence and safety assessment

**Route:** `/body-wont-settle/`  
**Review date:** 7 November 2026  
**Status:** internal research review only; no public copy changes; not published  
**Scope:** brief muscle release, comfortable breathing, and attention to surroundings. This is not a systematic review of every eligible trial.

## Overall decision

Keep all three visitor-facing exercises unchanged. The broader evidence supports offering relaxation approaches as optional choices, but none of the studies below directly validates the exact brief exercises on coping.tools. Do not interpret physiological changes, improvements against passive controls, or improvements within both groups as proof of a specific intervention effect.

## 1. Gentle muscle release

**Evidence classification: indirect support; some student-specific support for longer or repeated exercises.**

- Donato et al. (2026), systematic review/meta-analysis of 31 randomized controlled trials (2,277 participants): anxiety SMD −1.11 (95% CI −1.69 to −0.53), **I²=85%**. Very high heterogeneity; intervention formats, clinical populations and comparators vary. The review's inclusion focus was adult sleep quality; its anxiety finding cannot be applied quantitatively to a brief muscle-release prompt. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jpsychores.2026.112563
- Toussaint et al. (2021), 60 healthy undergraduates randomized to 20 minutes of recorded PMR, deep breathing, guided imagery or control. Relaxation interventions improved relaxation states relative to control. A 20-minute instructed session is substantially longer than the guide. https://doi.org/10.1155/2021/5924040
- Matsumoto & Smith (2001), 42 students randomized to PMR or breathing exercises practiced over five weeks. Different relaxation-state outcomes favoured different methods; there was no untreated comparison. https://doi.org/10.1002/jclp.1117
- Brief online relaxation trial in 114 college students: four-week abbreviated PMR improved five of six measured outcomes and differed from self-monitoring at posttest, **but not from mindful breathing**. Supports a short-format programme more closely than conventional PMR, but still not a single one-minute release. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35709245/
- Costa et al. (2022), university-student randomized relaxation trial with active relaxing-music and passive controls: the authors reported **increases in anxiety and stress** in the relaxation-training group, suggesting possible adverse or uncomfortable initial experiences. This must be retained as contradictory evidence, without generalising it to all relaxation methods. https://doi.org/10.14417/ap.1909

**Practical interpretation:** Keep the option to adapt, stop or skip the exercise. Do not claim PMR is always calming or imply the pooled effect size predicts benefit from this specific prompt.

## 2. Comfortable breathing

**Evidence classification: mixed indirect evidence; no direct validation of this guide's gentle breathing instruction.**

- Fincham et al. (2023), placebo-controlled randomized trial, N=400: ~10 minutes/day of coherent breathing at ~5.5 breaths/min versus credible paced breathing at 12 breaths/min for four weeks. Both groups improved over time, but the primary stress outcome showed **no between-group benefit** (group-by-time p=0.765); similar null comparisons at follow-up. This is important active-control evidence, not proof that breathing never helps. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-023-49279-8
- Fincham et al. (2023), meta-analysis of breathwork RCTs: some positive pooled findings, but comparison conditions, study quality and breathwork protocols differ. Interpret alongside the later credible-placebo null trial. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-022-27247-y
- Pilcher et al. (2025), randomized college-student study of brief slow-paced breathing and working memory, mood and stress. Relevant student population, but the studied protocol is not identical to an unpaced comfortable-breathing suggestion. https://doi.org/10.1080/10615806.2025.2505897

**Practical interpretation:** No fixed rate, breath-holding requirement, performance target or promise of a particular feeling should be inferred from these trials. Maintain comfort and choice, including stopping if the practice feels unpleasant.

## 3. Attention to surroundings

**Evidence classification: guidance-based rationale; insufficient direct evidence for the exact short prompt.**

- WHO, *Doing What Matters in Times of Stress*, includes grounding/attention-to-surroundings as a practical stress-management principle. Guidance supports plausibility and accessibility, **not clinical validation of the exact coping.tools exercise**. https://tdr.who.int/home/our-work/global-engagement/9789240003927
- Moser, Moran & Leber (2015), college-student experimental manipulation of attention to nonemotional visual distractors. This is an attentional laboratory task, **not sensory grounding**; do not cite it as direct proof of the guide's exercise. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.beth.2015.07.001

**Practical interpretation:** Describe the exercise as an optional way to direct attention outward, not as a treatment proven to reduce anxiety or stop physiological arousal.

## Editorial and safety implications

1. Preserve the existing three exercises, the immediate-medical-caution language, and optional/adaptable framing.
2. Preserve the negative coherent-breathing result and the new student-specific adverse/contradictory relaxation result in the internal record.
3. Do not add student-specific language to the universal guide.
4. Do not add more public citations merely to increase citation count; existing public evidence already covers major PMR and breathwork reviews.
5. Do not change the public last-reviewed date until a public evidence update is approved.
6. Before future publication, check primary full texts for attrition, outcome timing, adverse-event ascertainment, and risk of bias, especially the Costa trial and the 114-student abbreviated PMR trial.

**Decision:** Internal research record updated on a review branch. No public HTML, PDFs, deployment settings, or production files changed.

## Additional positive studies — focused verification, 7 November 2026

These studies strengthen the evidence for *related* relaxation and breathing techniques; none validates the exact short coping.tools exercises.

1. **Toussaint et al. (2021)**, randomized comparison, **60 undergraduate students**, single **20-minute** audio-guided progressive muscle relaxation, deep breathing, guided imagery, or control. All three interventions increased self-reported relaxation relative to control; PMR and guided imagery showed more immediate physiological relaxation patterns, while deep breathing initially increased physiological arousal before returning toward baseline. https://doi.org/10.1155/2021/5924040. **Applicability:** useful single-session support for the two underlying methods, but much longer than the route's prompts; the physiological results are not uniform.
2. **Balban et al. (2023)**, remote randomized trial comparing **daily five-minute** cyclic sighing, box breathing, cyclic hyperventilation with retention, and mindfulness meditation for **one month**. Cyclic sighing showed greater improvement in positive affect and reduction in respiratory rate than mindfulness meditation. Both meditation and breathwork groups improved state anxiety over time; **between-group superiority for anxiety was not established**. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.xcrm.2022.100895. **Applicability:** short self-guided daily practice, but prescribed patterns and repeated use; not the guide's comfortable unpaced breathing.
3. **Riedl et al. (2026)**, preregistered just-in-time pilot in **47 university students** during real-life acute threat situations. One-minute box breathing or cyclic sighing was associated with greater state-anxiety reductions than passive control at five minutes. Reaction times on an attention task were **slower** in both breathing conditions, despite fewer inhibition errors after box breathing. https://doi.org/10.1080/10615806.2026.2659809. **Applicability:** closest duration and acute context, but small pilot, passive control, and distinct techniques; findings do not justify adding breath holding or forced breathing to the guide.

**Evidence synthesis:** The positive studies support offering optional muscle relaxation and some breathing practices. Retain the credible-active-control null breathing trial and the contrary student relaxation result already recorded. No direct evidence was identified for this route's exact attention-to-surroundings instruction. No claim of equivalent efficacy for these shorter prompts is justified.

**Suggested concise public evidence wording (proposal only):** “Controlled studies have found that muscle relaxation and some brief breathing exercises can improve relaxation or reduce anxiety. However, the studies used particular techniques and durations, and they do not establish that these shorter exercises have the same effects.”

**Publication decision:** Keep this research assessment internal pending approval. Do not add techniques, change instructions, or publish on the strength of these studies alone.
