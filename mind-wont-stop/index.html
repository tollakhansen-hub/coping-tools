My mind won’t stop

Sometimes your mind keeps going over the same thing.

What happened. What might happen. What you should have done. What you need to figure out.

Not every thought needs an answer.

For the next minute

Catch one loop

Put the main thought into one short sentence.

“My mind keeps coming back to…”

One sentence is enough.

If a small useful action is obvious, write it down.

If it is not obvious, you do not need to work it out right now.

You can leave the sentence where it is. The later steps are optional.

If you have a few minutes

Give it somewhere to go later

If the same worry keeps returning, choose a short time later today when you can look at it properly.

Write down the thought.

Write down the time.

Then, if you can, return to what you were doing.

When the thought comes back, there is no need to argue with it or prove it wrong.

The note can hold it until then.

When that time comes, you can use “Sort it once” below to look for one useful next step.

If it helps, decide beforehand: “If the worry comes back before then, I’ll notice it and return to what I was doing.”

If setting the worry aside makes you more caught up in it, leave this exercise and try “Let the thought come along” below.

Or let the thought come along

A thought can be there while you do something else.

Notice that the thought is there.

Then choose one ordinary thing in front of you.

  • Make a drink.
  • Put something away.
  • Take a shower.
  • Finish one small task.

The thought can stay in the background while you do it.

The aim is not to force your mind to become quiet.

It is to give the thought a little less of your attention.

If you are still going over it later

Sort it once

Take a piece of paper or a note on your phone.

Write three short lines:

What do I actually know?

What am I predicting, replaying or trying to work out?

Is there one useful thing I can do next?

If there is an action, write it down.

If there is not, you can stop for now.

Close the note.

You can come back to it another time if you need to.

You can try one small next step without having everything settled.

If something else feels closer

  • My body won’t settle
  • There’s too much at once

Need support? If possible, reach out to someone you trust or a health professional. In immediate danger or a medical emergency? Contact local emergency services.

About this tool

Research in adults with diagnosed mental-health conditions supports targeting worry and rumination within cognitive behavioural approaches, though results vary. Worry postponement on its own has mixed evidence. These exact short exercises have not been clinically validated. A 2025 meta-analysis of 55 randomized trials found a moderate overall reduction in repetitive negative thinking. For worry postponement specifically, a meta-analysis found small short-term benefits, while a separate randomized trial found no between-group benefit. In a 2025 trial, adding an if–then plan reduced worry duration relative to standard postponement. However, the postponement groups combined did not reduce worry duration compared with an active control, sleep did not improve, and standard postponement increased reported worry frequency compared with the active control. The optional if–then example here is a practical adaptation, not a proven way to reduce worry. Drop postponement if it increases preoccupation.
