My mind won’t stop

Sometimes your mind keeps going over the same thing.

What happened. What might happen. What you should have done. What you need to figure out.

Not every thought needs an answer.

For the next minute

Catch one loop

Put the main thought into one sentence.

You can write it down or just say it quietly to yourself:

“My mind keeps coming back to…”

Finish the sentence once.

Then ask:

Does anything need doing about this in the next hour?

If yes, choose the smallest useful action.

If no, leave the sentence where it is.

You do not need to keep working on it right now.

If you have a few minutes

Give it somewhere to go later

If the same worry keeps returning, choose a short time later today when you can look at it properly.

Write down the thought.

Write down the time.

Then return to what you were doing.

When the thought comes back, there is no need to argue with it or prove it wrong.

You have already given it a place.

If postponing the thought makes you more preoccupied with it, stop using this tool.

Or let the thought come along

A thought can be there while you do something else.

Notice that the thought is there.

Then choose one ordinary thing in front of you.

  • Make a drink.
  • Put something away.
  • Take a shower.
  • Finish one small task.

Let the thought be in the background while you do it.

The aim is not to force your mind to become quiet.

It is to give the thought a little less of your attention.

If you are still going over it later

Sort it once

Take a piece of paper or a note on your phone.

Write three short lines:

What do I actually know?

What am I predicting, replaying or trying to work out?

Is there one useful thing I can do next?

If there is an action, write it down.

If there is not, you can stop for now.

Close the note.

You can come back to it another time if you need to.

You can carry on with your day without reaching certainty first.

If something else feels closer

  • There’s too much at once

About this tool

Research on repetitive negative thinking supports directly targeting worry and rumination within cognitive behavioural approaches; a 2025 meta-analysis of 55 randomized studies found a moderate overall reduction. Worry postponement on its own has mixed evidence, so it is optional here and should be dropped if it increases preoccupation. The exact short exercises on this page have not been clinically validated.
