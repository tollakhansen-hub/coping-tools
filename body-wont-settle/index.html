My body won’t settle

Sometimes your body stays tense, shaky, restless or on alert even when you want it to calm down.

The feeling does not have to disappear. Pick one thing that feels manageable.

For the next minute

Release three places

Press your feet gently into the floor for a few seconds.

Let go.

Lift your shoulders slightly.

Let go.

Gently tighten your hands.

Let go.

Notice the difference between holding tension and releasing it.

You can stop there.

If you have a few minutes

Try a slower breath

Only use this if paying attention to your breathing feels comfortable.

Let your breathing stay gentle rather than taking very large breaths.

Breathe in comfortably.

Let the breath out slowly.

Continue for a few minutes if it is helping.

If focusing on your breathing makes you feel more uncomfortable, stop. Try the outside-world exercise instead.

Put some attention outside your body

Look around you.

Choose one ordinary object.

Notice its shape.

Notice two small details you had not paid attention to before.

Then find another object and do the same.

There is nothing you need to achieve. You are simply giving your attention somewhere concrete.

If your body is still wound up later

Try a longer round of gentle muscle release

Work through a few areas of your body one at a time: hands, shoulders, legs and feet.

Gently tense one area.

Hold briefly.

Release fully.

Pause before moving to the next area.

Do not strain or tense anything that hurts.

Try not to keep checking whether you feel calm yet.

A small reduction in tension is enough.

This is a coping tool, not a way to decide whether physical symptoms are medical. If a symptom is new, severe or medically concerning, get appropriate medical help instead of assuming it is anxiety.

About this tool

Progressive muscle relaxation has direct support across adult studies for reducing stress and anxiety. Breathwork trials show small-to-moderate average benefits for stress, but study quality and methods vary. The short exercises on this page are practical adaptations; this exact combination has not been tested as a clinical intervention.
