My body won’t settle

Sometimes your body stays tense, shaky, restless or on alert even when you want it to calm down.

The feeling does not have to disappear. Pick one thing that feels manageable.

For the next minute

Release three places

Use gentle pressure only. Skip any movement that hurts.

Press your feet gently into the floor for a few seconds.

Let go.

Lift your shoulders slightly.

Let go.

Gently tighten your hands.

Let go.

You do not need to check whether it made a difference.

You can stop there. The later steps are optional.

If you have a few minutes

Try a slower breath

Only use this if paying attention to your breathing feels comfortable.

Let your breathing stay gentle rather than taking very large breaths.

Breathe in comfortably.

Let the breath out slowly.

Continue for a few minutes if it is helping.

If focusing on your breathing makes you feel more uncomfortable, stop. Try the outside-world exercise instead.

Put some attention outside your body

Look around you.

Choose one ordinary object.

Notice its shape.

Notice two small details you had not paid attention to before.

Then find another object and do the same.

There is nothing you need to achieve. You are simply giving your attention somewhere concrete.

If something around you is adding to the tension, you could change one small thing — such as moving somewhere quieter — if that feels possible and safe.

If your body is still wound up later

Try a longer round of gentle muscle release

Work through a few areas of your body one at a time: hands, shoulders, legs and feet.

Gently tense one area.

Hold briefly.

Let the tension ease.

Pause before moving to the next area.

Skip any area that hurts or feels wrong.

You do not need to keep checking whether you feel calm.

You can stop after the round, whether or not you notice a change.

If something else feels closer

  • My mind won’t stop
  • There’s too much at once

Need support? If possible, reach out to someone you trust or a health professional. In immediate danger or a medical emergency? Contact local emergency services.

About this tool

These options draw on research into muscle relaxation, breathing and WHO stress-management guidance. The exact short steps here have not been tested as a package. Fuller muscle-relaxation programs have evidence for anxiety, but results vary across studies. Breathing research also varies by method; evidence for box breathing or cyclic sighing does not establish that this gentler prompt works.

A small 2026 pilot in 47 university students found greater short-term anxiety reductions after about one minute of box breathing or cyclic sighing than after a passive control condition. Participants received instruction and practice beforehand. This is preliminary evidence for those techniques, not validation of this page’s gentle breathing exercise.

A single-session randomized study in 60 university students found increased relaxation after 20 minutes of muscle relaxation or deep breathing compared with a control group. A separate month-long trial found that daily five-minute structured breathing, especially cyclic sighing, improved positive mood and respiratory rate compared with mindfulness meditation. These findings support related approaches, but not the exact shorter exercises on this page.

The outside-world exercise reflects the grounding principle of attending to your surroundings in WHO’s stress-management guidance. The cited guidance does not clinically validate this exact exercise.

A 2026 meta-analysis of 31 trials found lower anxiety after progressive muscle relaxation, but results varied substantially between studies (I²=85%). It studied fuller programs, not this short release. A small study of box breathing and cyclic sighing likewise does not validate the page’s gentler breathing prompt.
