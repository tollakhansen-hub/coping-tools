Something has happened

Bad news, a loss or a sudden change can leave you upset, numb, confused or unsure what to do next. Making sense of everything can wait.

For the next minute

Check the next practical need

If you are in immediate danger, get to safety or seek urgent help first.

Is there one practical need to attend to in the next hour?

  • Get somewhere you need to be.
  • Contact someone you trust.
  • Have something to eat or drink.
  • Find one piece of information you need.

If nothing comes to mind, you can pause. The later steps are optional.

If you have a few minutes

Separate now from later

If several things need attention, you could jot down what needs doing soon and what can wait. One or two items is enough.

  • Now: what needs attention in the next few hours, such as a phone call or getting somewhere.
  • Later: something that can wait.

If there’s a Now item, you can start with one small step or ask someone to help. Leave the Later item where you can find it when you have more room.

You do not have to talk about it

You might want company, want to talk, or prefer some quiet. Any of those can be reasonable.

You can ask someone you trust to help with one practical thing, without explaining everything.

Choose when to look again

If the event is something you are following in the news or online, you can choose when you will look again.

Until then, you can close the feed.

If you need updates for your safety, keep those available.

Closing it is not pretending the event did not happen. It simply stops the same material arriving continuously.

For the rest of today

Keep one ordinary thing

If it feels manageable, keep one familiar thing from your usual day.

  • Make your usual drink.
  • Eat something familiar.
  • Take a shower.
  • Walk the dog.
  • Put on clean clothes.
  • Go through part of your normal evening.

One familiar activity can give this part of the day a known shape. You can leave making sense of everything for later.

You do not need to make sense of it now

You can stop here. You do not need a complete plan right now.

If something else feels closer

  • My mind won’t stop
  • My body won’t settle

Need support? If possible, reach out to someone you trust or a health professional. In immediate danger or a medical emergency? Contact local emergency services.

About this tool

WHO and Red Cross guidance recommend practical, respectful support after distressing events: attend to immediate needs, help people connect with support and let them choose whether to talk. This guide adapts those principles to focus on what is needed now; its effects as a self-guided sequence have not been established. This is guidance for present support, not evidence that these steps prevent PTSD or other later mental-health problems.

Psychological first-aid outcome research remains limited and mixed. A small 2026 laboratory trial randomized 84 people after distressing video exposure. The control group had greater immediate stress reduction, and there was no overall group difference at two weeks. Higher initial distress was linked with greater later trauma symptoms only in the group-PFA arm, raising a possible concern. This study did not test practical one-to-one support after a real event.

NICE recommends against psychologically focused debriefing for PTSD prevention or treatment. This does not prohibit ordinary supportive conversations when someone wants to talk.

Research links greater mass-trauma news exposure with later symptoms, but it is observational. Choosing when to check updates is a practical option rather than a proven treatment; keep information needed for safety available.
