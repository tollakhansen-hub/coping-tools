There’s too much at once

When several things are pulling at you at the same time, even deciding where to start can add to the pressure.

Organising everything can wait.

Make the field smaller.

For the next minute

Put one thing somewhere else

Take a piece of paper or open a blank note.

Write down one thing that is demanding your attention.

One short line is enough. You do not need to choose the most important thing.

If an obvious next-hour action comes to mind, write only that action underneath.

If nothing is obvious, you can stop there.

The rest does not need sorting yet.

If you have a few minutes

Make one thing smaller

Choose one thing you intend to do.

Instead of asking:

How do I get this finished?

ask:

What is the first small thing I would physically do?

“Sort out the bills” might become:

Find the bill with the nearest due date.

“Deal with that email” might become:

Open the email and read it once.

“Get the house sorted” might become:

Clear one surface.

If the first step still feels too large, make it smaller again.

Doing the first step is enough for now.

Give one thing a later place

Choose something that does not need to happen immediately.

Give it a specific later place.

For example:

Tomorrow after breakfast — call the dentist.

or

Friday — look at the insurance letter.

Put it in your calendar, reminders or notes if that helps.

Then leave it there.

The reminder can hold it for now, if that helps.

If the whole day feels overloaded

Reduce the load, not just the feeling

Look again at what is asking something from you.

Is there one thing that can be:

  • later
  • smaller
  • not done today

Choose one.

You are not trying to create the perfect plan.

You can decide what does not need to compete for your attention right now.

Then, if you can, return to the next small thing in front of you.

If something else feels closer

  • My mind won’t stop
  • I feel low or shut down

About this tool

Research comparing multitasking with single-tasking finds greater physiological stress-system activation during multitasking. Separate research on intention offloading supports reminders as an effective way to hold delayed intentions outside memory. Neither finding shows that this page treats overwhelm; they support the practical ideas of narrowing demands and externalizing what can wait.
