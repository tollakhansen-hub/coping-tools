### Amanvermez et al. estimates and bias

- Positive Hedges’ g favours the programmes. General samples, pooled across outcomes at post-test: g = 0.19 (95% CI 0.10 to 0.29), I² = 48% (95% CI 19% to 66%). Stress alone: g = 0.25 (0.15 to 0.35), I² = 53% (28% to 70%). Attention-control subgroup: g = 0.10 (−0.03 to 0.23). The review found no significant moderator effect for comparator type.
- Elevated-stress samples, pooled across outcomes at post-test: g = 0.34 (0.16 to 0.52), I² = 0% (0% to 79%). Stress alone: g = 0.27 (−0.04 to 0.58), I² = 56% (0% to 88%). The two populations were analysed separately. The difference in their pooled estimates does not establish greater effectiveness in elevated-stress students.
- Follow-up estimates pooled across outcomes: general samples up to six months, g = 0.01 (−0.10 to 0.12), 12 comparisons. Elevated-stress samples up to three months, g = 0.31 (0.08 to 0.53), three studies. Follow-up data were sparse.
- RoB 2 assessment omitted the outcome-measurement domain and used the authors’ overall rules. Ratings in general samples were 11 low, 10 with some concerns and five high. Elevated-stress studies were one low, two with some concerns and one high. Roughly 28% of participants lacked complete post-test data. Funnel plot, Egger and trim-and-fill signals differed. Four elevated-stress studies offer very limited information about small-study effects and heterogeneity.

## 25 October 2026 — Four-route focused evidence and usability review

A PFA systematic review (PMID 36300605) found limited/heterogeneous outcome evidence, and a 2026 *group* PFA video-analogue trial (PMID 42561812) found no overall two-week benefit and an adverse-direction subgroup result; neither tests this route's simple text. Preserve existing caveats and avoid PTSD-prevention promises. Add a safety-first sentence and express that help can be practical without retelling an event.

### Questions and search record
- Questions: what supports worry postponement on its own; how closely do studied interventions match this page; what conflicting findings or practical gaps affect the user?
- Search date: 25 October 2026. Used web search to locate PubMed/PMC records, publisher articles and author/university repositories; followed the route's existing citations. No direct exhaustive PsycINFO, CENTRAL or trial-registry search was performed.
- Queries included `worry postponement randomized trial systematic review 2025 2026 repetitive negative thinking`, `brief cognitive defusion worry rumination randomized trial single session`, the exact titles of the McCarrick and Versluis trials, `Worry Postponement From the Metacognitive Perspective`, and the Stenzel corrigendum PMID 41592808.
- Prioritised systematic reviews and controlled outcome studies relevant to worry, rumination, postponement and brief self-help; retained null and adverse-direction findings. ### Evidence decisions
- Dippel, Brosschot and Verkuil, DOI: https://doi.org/10.1007/s41811-023-00193-x. Full-text PDF inspected: https://www.piekeren.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/03/Dippel.2023_Worry-postponement-meta-analysis.pdf. Seven randomized trials, 999 participants; small short-term effects versus worry registration. Practice extended over days/weeks; long-term follow-up was lacking. Supports cautious background wording, not immediate benefit from the site's exact exercise.
- Versluis et al., PMID: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26511764/. Abstract reviewed. Online trial recruited 996 people; 361 completed. No between-group benefit for worry or subjective health complaints. High attrition and the online setting matter when applying results to unguided self-help. Retain mixed-evidence wording.
- McCarrick et al., DOI: https://doi.org/10.1080/08870446.2025.2590072. Full text inspected through https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/235230/. Confirmed the earlier record's distinction between benefit versus standard postponement and null results versus active control, and the higher reported worry frequency in standard postponement versus active control (Table 3). Retain stop/switch permission; this is not proof of harm from the site's adaptation.
- Important intervention mismatch: the McCarrick if–then plans tied the scheduled worry period to a habitual activity and included adherence checks. The site's example instead cues noticing the returning worry and resuming the current activity. It is not the tested planning procedure. Retain explicit adaptation status; do not attribute the trial's effect to this example or claim superiority for it.
- Krzikalla et al. (2024), DOI: https://doi.org/10.32872/cpe.12741; PMID: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39119056/. Abstract and accessible indexed methods/discussion reviewed; full-text retrieval was intermittent. Relevant study absent from the public reference list: 47 participants with GAD and 35 with hypochondriasis, two therapist sessions with six days of practice, waitlist comparison. The significant post-hoc between-group worry benefit was in GAD; results for hypochondriasis were limited. Small diagnostic subgroups, self-report, therapist involvement and passive comparator prevent treating this as validation of the brief site exercise. Record as background; no additional public paragraph needed.
- Stenzel et al., original DOI: https://doi.org/10.1017/S0033291725000017; correction: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12902165/. Original publisher text and full correction inspected. Correction changes subgroup classifications/estimates; overall moderate effect remains. Public overall summary remains appropriate. This is a recheck of a previously recorded correction, not a newly discovered correction.
- WHO guidance remains principle-level support rather than validation of this page's exact noticing/action prompts. The exploratory brief-defusion search did not establish validation of the exact exercises. Absence of validation in this focused search is not proof that no relevant study exists.

### Approved practical changes
- Add a brief connection from the scheduled time to the existing “Sort it once” exercise so the visitor has a next step when that time arrives.
- Replace the postponement stop instruction with plain language and an explicit pointer to “Let the thought come along”. This offers an alternative, not a claim that it is more effective or safer for everyone.
- These are editorial usability decisions, not trial-tested modifications. Preserve the remaining exercises, optional if–then example, public evidence summary, references and layout. Update this route's review date and sitemap modification date only.
- Keep the other exercises: this focused check found no basis for removing them, but does not establish their efficacy. Do not infer whole-route safety or effectiveness from evidence for broader treatments.

## 8 October 2026 — targeted research update

Scope: current five route pages and targeted 2025–2026 literature search; not a systematic review. Accessible full texts and abstracts were distinguished. No study below validates the exact coping.tools exercises. All five public review dates updated after this targeted review.

### My body won’t settle — breathing and muscle relaxation
- McAllister et al. (2026), DOI: 10.1016/j.cpnec.2026.100360; PMID: 42388906; PMC13321280. Full text examined. Randomized laboratory trial, 66 adults aged 18–39, excluding diagnosed psychological conditions. Box breathing and prolonged exhalation attenuated some acute stress responses; cortisol, HRV and cognitive performance did not significantly differ across conditions. Baseline anxiety differed between groups, limiting interpretation. This does not establish effects in clinical populations or of the site's gentle untimed breathing exercise.

Abstract-level review: daily-life slow-paced breathing associated with lower perceived stress versus control times; greater reported benefit when experienced as natural. - Li et al. (2026), DOI: 10.3389/fpubh.2026.1906525. Accessible full text reviewed: 14 randomized trials, 957 adults, subjective sleep-quality outcome, high heterogeneity (I² 85.5%); effects in adults aged 55+ uncertain. Supports fuller PMR protocols for this outcome, not the one-minute three-area adaptation or immediate distress relief.
- Decision: public exercise and evidence wording unchanged; existing distinction between protocols, guidance and exact adaptations retained.

### My mind won’t stop — worry postponement with planning
- McCarrick et al. (2025), DOI: 10.1080/08870446.2025.2590072; PMID: 41347618. Abstract reviewed: four-arm randomized trial, 186 participants, 14 days. Augmented if–then planning reduced worry duration by approximately 15 minutes relative to standard postponement, not a demonstrated 15-minute benefit over every control. No significant sleep improvements versus controls.
- Decision: add an optional if–then example to the existing postponement step, retain stop permission and mixed-evidence wording. Additive example is an untested site adaptation; does not establish improved sleep or immediate relief.

### There’s too much at once — trusted reminders
- Dupre and colleagues (2026), *Let it go: How trusted reminders alter intention maintenance*, DOI: 10.3758/s13423-026-02985-6; PMID: 42613406. Accessible full text reviewed. Experiment in 320 students: initial reminders improved performance without reducing intention-related thoughts; after trust-building, fully reliable reminders reduced intention-related thoughts and increased ongoing-task thoughts. Unexpected absence of trusted reminders could impair performance. Design not preregistered; memory/attention outcomes, not emotional overwhelm.
- Decision: preserve conditional reminder wording. Practical implication is to use a reminder the person can find and rely on; no claim that a written note automatically clears attention or treats distress.

### Persistent stress — nurse-led collaborative care versus guided self-help CBT
- Source: Kontio et al. (2026), PMID: 42843928; DOI: 10.1136/bmjment-2026-302797. https://mentalhealth.bmj.com/content/29/1/e302797
- Population and intervention: 172 patients at one Swedish primary-care centre with mild-to-moderate stress-related difficulties; nurse-led collaborative care (2–6 visits over up to 12 weeks) versus 12-week psychologist-guided self-help CBT.
- Outcomes: small primary between-group difference favouring CBT (d=0.22); one-sided CI upper bound 0.52 exceeded the prespecified non-inferiority margin of 0.3. Non-inferiority was not demonstrated; this does not establish that collaborative care is ineffective. Both groups improved, with improvements sustained at one year.
- Limitations: no untreated control, self-report outcomes, single centre, predominantly middle-aged well-educated women; no formal fidelity ratings; no complementary per-protocol analysis.
- Cautions: negative experiences were reported during treatment in both groups, commonly increased stress; no serious negative effects reported. These descriptive reports do not establish comparative harm or harm from a site exercise.
- Relevance: background for collaborative problem solving on “There’s too much at once”; not evidence for the exact short adaptation. Persistent stress is not equivalent to early post-trauma intervention.
- Decision: no public change. Keep treatment evidence separate from practical principle and exact-exercise evidence.

### REPICAL — guided digital multicomponent stepped care
- Source: González-Spinoglio et al. (2026), PMID: 42829513; DOI: 10.1155/da/5875889. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13633456/
- Population and intervention: 165 distressed long-term-care workers in Catalonia; both groups received 15–20 minutes of psychological first aid (PFA). The intervention group additionally received five weeks of Doing What Matters (DWM), including weekly 15-minute helper calls, followed when distress persisted by five weekly 60-minute individual Problem Management Plus (PM+) sessions.
- Outcomes: week-21 PHQ-ADS difference 4.2 points (95% CI 1.1–7.3), favouring the programme; no significant health-related quality-of-life improvement. Of 62 intervention participants assessed after DWM, 57 stepped up to PM+.
- Limitations: 165 recruited versus target 212; 22.4% attrition, higher in intervention group; self-report, unblinded participants, predominantly women, short follow-up. Package design does not isolate DWM, PM+, grounding, activation, problem solving or helper contact.
- Cautions: ten participants with suicidal ideation were referred to mental-health services; none assessed as high/imminent risk. No other serious adverse events reported; events were not considered participation-related. This is not a safety test of unguided site exercises.
- Contradictory/context evidence: the discussion describes mixed earlier standalone DWM findings; programme benefit must not be translated into certainty about standalone DWM or a one-minute adaptation.
- Relevance: indirect background across grounding, problem solving and activation. Shared PFA in both arms means this contrast cannot establish PFA efficacy.
- Decision: no public change or stronger efficacy claim. Preserve guided-treatment versus short self-help boundary.

## 13 October 2026 — brief breathing and worry evidence clarification

Scope: targeted follow-up to the five-route review. Public changes are limited to evidence notes, references and review dates on the body and mind routes. Practical instructions are unchanged. Other route dates remain 8 October because no public evidence update was required. This is not a systematic review.

### My body won’t settle — one-minute breathing pilot
- Riedl et al. (2026), DOI: 10.1080/10615806.2026.2659809. Published online 19 April 2026; newly considered here, not newly published since 8 October. Accessible full text reviewed via the authors’ university repository.
- Preregistered within-person field experiment in 47 university students aged 18–30 (43 women); 782 retained pre/post anxiety observations. Approximately one minute of box breathing or cyclic sighing compared with a passive usual-activity condition, with anxiety assessed five minutes after the initial questionnaire. Both techniques showed greater short-term anxiety reductions than control.
- Participants received initial instruction, six practice exercises and video guidance. Certain medical conditions and pregnancy were excluded. Small, selected sample, passive comparator, self-report outcome and short outcome interval limit generalization and specificity. Repeated observations do not constitute 782 independent participants.
- Attention outcomes were mixed: fewer inhibition errors after box breathing, but longer reaction times after both techniques. Do not claim improved concentration or productivity. No conclusion about long-term benefit or comprehensive safety can be drawn.
- Decision: add a cautious public evidence paragraph and reference. No change to gentle untimed breathing, no breath holds or mandatory timers. The study does not validate the site's exercise.

### My mind won’t stop — full-text check of postponement trial
- McCarrick et al. (2025), DOI: 10.1080/08870446.2025.2590072; PMID: 41347618. Accessible full text reviewed via White Rose repository, extending the 8 October abstract-level review. Four-arm online randomized trial, 186 participants over 14 days; daily self-report measures and substantial missing observations limit interpretation.
- Augmented if–then postponement reduced worry duration relative to standard postponement by approximately 15 minutes. This is not a demonstrated benefit over active control. Combined intervention arms did not significantly reduce worry duration versus active control. Sleep outcomes did not improve. Standard postponement increased reported worry frequency versus active control; do not conceal this adverse-direction result or describe it as established clinical harm.
- Decision: explicitly describe the comparator, null results and increased-frequency finding in the public evidence note, and add the trial link. Keep the optional practical example and stop instruction unchanged; the site adaptation remains unvalidated.

### My mind won’t stop — corrigendum verified and linked
- Stenzel et al., DOI: 10.1017/S0033291726103304; PMID: 41592808. Full publisher correction reviewed (Cambridge University Press, 28 January 2026). Already recorded internally on 30 September; this is not a new correction.
- Reclassification moves metacognitive therapy, Applied Relaxation and one Emotion Regulation Therapy treatment into the RNT-specific subgroup. Corrected subgroup effects: g = −0.87 versus −0.48; difference 0.39. Authors report conclusions unchanged.
- Decision: retain the public overall moderate-effect summary, which does not quote affected subgroup estimates; add a public corrigendum link alongside the original meta-analysis.
