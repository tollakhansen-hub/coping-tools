## 30 September 2026

### There’s too much at once — cognitive offloading
- Reviewed: Burnett LK, Richmond LL. *Meta-analytic investigations of the effect of cognitive offloading on memory-based task performance and interindividual variability*. Memory & Cognition. 2026;54(1):144–168. PMID: 40500483.
- Decision: use this newer meta-analysis as the main synthesis for cognitive offloading instead of the older review previously linked on the page.
- Interpretation: evidence supports cognitive offloading for memory-based task performance and reduced reliance on internal memory; it does not establish that writing a task down treats emotional overwhelm.
- Public page wording remains intentionally cautious.

### Something has happened — mass-trauma media exposure
- Reviewed: Pfefferbaum B, Nitiéma P, Newman E. *Is Viewing Mass Trauma Television Coverage Associated With Trauma Reactions in Adults and Youth? A Meta-Analytic Review*. Journal of Traumatic Stress. 2019;32(2):175–185. PMID: 30913350.
- Decision: replace reliance on the single Boston Marathon observational study with this broader meta-analysis.
- Interpretation: greater mass-trauma media exposure is associated with post-traumatic and acute stress symptoms; the evidence is observational and does not prove that reducing exposure treats distress.

### Something has happened — psychological first aid
- Reviewed: Hermosilla S, Forthal S, Sadowska K, et al. *We need to build the evidence: A systematic review of psychological first aid on mental health and well-being*. Journal of Traumatic Stress. 2023;36(1):5–16. PMID: 36300605.
- Decision: add this systematic review alongside WHO psychological-first-aid guidance.
- Interpretation: PFA principles are widely endorsed and practical, but outcome evidence is limited, heterogeneous and at generally high risk of bias. The site should use PFA as guidance for humane/practical principles, not claim it prevents PTSD or other later mental-health problems.

### My mind won’t stop — 2026 corrigendum
- Original review: Stenzel KL, Keller J, Kirchner L, Rief W. *Efficacy of cognitive behavioral therapy in treating repetitive negative thinking, rumination, and worry — a transdiagnostic meta-analysis*. Psychological Medicine. 2025;55:e31. PMID: 39916353.
- Corrigendum: Psychological Medicine. 2026;56:e31. PMID: 41592808. DOI: 10.1017/S0033291726103304.
- Decision: record the correction; no change to the public summary is required.
- Reason: the corrigendum changed classification/subgroup estimates but did not overturn the overall conclusion that CBT interventions reduce repetitive negative thinking. The public page only states the overall moderate reduction and therefore remains defensible.

## 8 October 2026 — targeted research update

Scope: current five route pages and targeted 2025–2026 literature search; not a systematic review. Accessible full texts and abstracts were distinguished. No study below validates the exact coping.tools exercises. All five public review dates updated after this targeted review.

### My body won’t settle — breathing and muscle relaxation
- McAllister et al. (2026), DOI: 10.1016/j.cpnec.2026.100360; PMID: 42388906; PMC13321280. Full text examined. Randomized laboratory trial, 66 adults aged 18–39, excluding diagnosed psychological conditions. Box breathing and prolonged exhalation attenuated some acute stress responses; cortisol, HRV and cognitive performance did not significantly differ across conditions. Baseline anxiety differed between groups, limiting interpretation. This does not establish effects in clinical populations or of the site's gentle untimed breathing exercise.
- Bamert et al. (2026), DOI: 10.1080/08870446.2026.2653702; PMID: 41972357. Abstract-level review: daily-life slow-paced breathing associated with lower perceived stress versus control times; greater reported benefit when experienced as natural. HRV did not significantly differ from control times. Preserve optional use and stop/switch permission; no physiological-reset claims.
- Li et al. (2026), DOI: 10.3389/fpubh.2026.1906525. Accessible full text reviewed: 14 randomized trials, 957 adults, subjective sleep-quality outcome, high heterogeneity (I² 85.5%); effects in adults aged 55+ uncertain. Supports fuller PMR protocols for this outcome, not the one-minute three-area adaptation or immediate distress relief.
- Decision: public exercise and evidence wording unchanged; existing distinction between protocols, guidance and exact adaptations retained.

### My mind won’t stop — worry postponement with planning
- McCarrick et al. (2025), DOI: 10.1080/08870446.2025.2590072; PMID: 41347618. Abstract reviewed: four-arm randomized trial, 186 participants, 14 days. Augmented if–then planning reduced worry duration by approximately 15 minutes relative to standard postponement, not a demonstrated 15-minute benefit over every control. No significant sleep improvements versus controls.
- Decision: add an optional if–then example to the existing postponement step, retain stop permission and mixed-evidence wording. Additive example is an untested site adaptation; does not establish improved sleep or immediate relief.

### There’s too much at once — trusted reminders
- Dupre and colleagues (2026), *Let it go: How trusted reminders alter intention maintenance*, DOI: 10.3758/s13423-026-02985-6; PMID: 42613406. Accessible full text reviewed. Experiment in 320 students: initial reminders improved performance without reducing intention-related thoughts; after trust-building, fully reliable reminders reduced intention-related thoughts and increased ongoing-task thoughts. Unexpected absence of trusted reminders could impair performance. Design not preregistered; memory/attention outcomes, not emotional overwhelm.
- Decision: preserve conditional reminder wording. Practical implication is to use a reminder the person can find and rely on; no claim that a written note automatically clears attention or treats distress.

### I feel low or shut down — brief action planning
- Ghosh, Kaveladze and Schleider (2026), DOI: 10.1177/21677026261470339; PMID: 42626677; PMC13492520. Accessible full text reviewed. Preregistered factorial experiment, 889 participants; action-plan component main effects on depressive symptoms were very small (SMD −0.18 at two weeks, −0.12 at eight weeks). Study used α=.10; interpret modest results cautiously. Structured scheduling of pleasurable/mastery activities differs from the site's ordinary coping examples.
- Decision: add optional action-and-time example; add evidence link and brief public evidence qualification. Retain scope detail for the existing Cuijpers review (105 trials overall, 61 main adult-outpatient comparisons) and explicit non-treatment boundary. No claim of validation for the site's exercise.

### Something has happened — contradictory PFA evidence and media exposure
- Devilly et al. (2026), DOI: 10.1016/j.brat.2026.105124; PMID: 42561812. Abstract reviewed; full publisher text unavailable. Randomized group-PFA stress-video analogue, 84 randomized, 74 followed up. Controls had greater immediate stress reduction; no overall group differences at two weeks. Higher initial distress was associated with greater later trauma symptoms only in the PFA arm, raising a potential harm signal. Small analogue trial and subgroup finding do not establish harm from this site's practical self-help checklist or all forms of PFA.
- Decision: explicitly add contradictory finding to public evidence paragraph and link. Keep voluntary support, no forced retelling, practical needs and no prevention claim.
- *When war sneaks in through the window* (2026), PMID: 42593982. Abstract reviewed: 389 Israeli adults followed weekly for six weeks; media-related distress and PTSD symptoms showed reciprocal associations. Observational, context-specific evidence; cannot establish that closing a feed treats distress.
- Decision: retain optional screen break and current observational-evidence limitation.


## 9 October 2026 — Val Town flagged trials, full-paper review

Scope: two flagged randomized trials; main full texts reviewed, supplementary files not separately appraised. Decision: retain as indirect background evidence for longer guided programmes. No public route wording, exercises, links or review dates changed.

### Persistent stress — nurse-led collaborative care versus guided self-help CBT
- Source: Kontio et al. (2026), PMID: 42843928; DOI: 10.1136/bmjment-2026-302797. https://mentalhealth.bmj.com/content/29/1/e302797
- Population and intervention: 172 patients at one Swedish primary-care centre with mild-to-moderate stress-related difficulties; nurse-led collaborative care (2–6 visits over up to 12 weeks) versus 12-week psychologist-guided self-help CBT.
- Outcomes: small primary between-group difference favouring CBT (d=0.22); one-sided CI upper bound 0.52 exceeded the prespecified non-inferiority margin of 0.3. Non-inferiority was not demonstrated; this does not establish that collaborative care is ineffective. Both groups improved, with improvements sustained at one year.
- Limitations: no untreated control, self-report outcomes, single centre, predominantly middle-aged well-educated women; no formal fidelity ratings; no complementary per-protocol analysis.
- Cautions: negative experiences were reported during treatment in both groups, commonly increased stress; no serious negative effects reported. These descriptive reports do not establish comparative harm or harm from a site exercise.
- Relevance: background for collaborative problem solving on “There’s too much at once”; not evidence for the exact short adaptation. Persistent stress is not equivalent to early post-trauma intervention.
- Decision: no public change. Keep treatment evidence separate from practical principle and exact-exercise evidence.

### REPICAL — guided digital multicomponent stepped care
- Source: González-Spinoglio et al. (2026), PMID: 42829513; DOI: 10.1155/da/5875889. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13633456/
- Population and intervention: 165 distressed long-term-care workers in Catalonia; both groups received 15–20 minutes of psychological first aid (PFA). The intervention group additionally received five weeks of Doing What Matters (DWM), including weekly 15-minute helper calls, followed when distress persisted by five weekly 60-minute individual Problem Management Plus (PM+) sessions.
- Outcomes: week-21 PHQ-ADS difference 4.2 points (95% CI 1.1–7.3), favouring the programme; no significant health-related quality-of-life improvement. Of 62 intervention participants assessed after DWM, 57 stepped up to PM+.
- Limitations: 165 recruited versus target 212; 22.4% attrition, higher in intervention group; self-report, unblinded participants, predominantly women, short follow-up. Package design does not isolate DWM, PM+, grounding, activation, problem solving or helper contact.
- Cautions: ten participants with suicidal ideation were referred to mental-health services; none assessed as high/imminent risk. No other serious adverse events reported; events were not considered participation-related. This is not a safety test of unguided site exercises.
- Contradictory/context evidence: the discussion describes mixed earlier standalone DWM findings; programme benefit must not be translated into certainty about standalone DWM or a one-minute adaptation.
- Relevance: indirect background across grounding, problem solving and activation. Shared PFA in both arms means this contrast cannot establish PFA efficacy.
- Decision: no public change or stronger efficacy claim. Preserve guided-treatment versus short self-help boundary.
